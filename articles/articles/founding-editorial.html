Founding Editorial · Volume 1, Number 1

The New Press: Artificial Intelligence, the Democratization of Scholarly Knowledge, and the Founding of This Journal

Kevin A. Schulte, Ph.D.

Founding Editor · August 2026

"The gate that remains — the only gate that should have existed all along — is the quality of the thinking."

I. The Gutenberg Moment

Johannes Gutenberg did not invent a single new idea. Let that fact be the first sentence of this journal, because it is the most important sentence for understanding what this journal is. The printing press, developed in Mainz during the 1440s and producing its first major work — the forty-two-line Bible — by approximately 1454 or 1455, was not an intellectual achievement in the way that Copernican heliocentrism or Newtonian mechanics were intellectual achievements. It was a production achievement. It solved an engineering problem: how to reproduce text in quantity, at speed, at a cost that made wide distribution not merely possible but inevitable. The ideas that followed the press did not emerge from the press. They emerged from human minds that had always possessed them, or that were now able to encounter the ideas of other human minds far more rapidly than the manuscript economy had permitted. Gutenberg gave the world a machine. The world then had to decide what to do with it. The world decided, within a generation, to print everything.

The mechanics of the pre-press bottleneck deserve precise description, because imprecision about the bottleneck leads to imprecision about what removing it actually accomplished. Before approximately 1440, the reproduction of a written text in Europe required one thing above all others: access to a scriptorium. The great scriptoria of the Benedictine monasteries — Monte Cassino, Saint Gall, Winchester, Cluny — were not incidental features of medieval intellectual life. They were the infrastructure of knowledge. A trained scribe could produce perhaps four pages of finished manuscript per day under favorable conditions, working from an existing exemplar. A substantial theological treatise of three hundred pages required something on the order of seventy-five days of skilled labor, not counting the preparation of vellum, the mixing and application of pigments for illumination, the binding, or the correction of inevitable errors. The cost of a single manuscript, measured in the market value of skilled labor alone, was prohibitive for all but the wealthiest institutional patrons — cathedral chapters, royal courts, large monasteries. The university book trade of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries developed the pecia system — a rental and supervised copying arrangement — precisely because the economics of manuscript production were otherwise incompatible with the scale of demand that universities generated. Knowledge reproduction was not merely expensive. It was structurally restricted to institutions that controlled the production apparatus.

The consequence of this structural restriction was not merely that fewer books existed. The consequence was that the gate between having an idea and disseminating it was controlled by the scribal class and by the institutional patrons who employed them. The Church did not censor manuscripts because it was uniquely authoritarian in its instincts. It controlled manuscripts because it controlled the scriptoria that produced them. The authority over ideas was downstream of the authority over production. Remove the production monopoly, and the authority over ideas followed it into obsolescence.

What happened next is not a matter of historical dispute, though its speed continues to astonish. By 1500 — within approximately fifty years of Gutenberg's Bible — the presses of Western Europe had produced more than twenty million volumes. By some estimates this figure exceeded the total number of manuscripts that European scribes had produced in the preceding one thousand years of production. Within twenty years of Gutenberg, printing operations had spread to roughly two hundred and seventy cities across Europe, from Mainz to Venice, from Paris to Kraków. The Luther controversy, which in 1517 began as a local academic dispute about indulgence practices, became a continental political crisis within three years — not because Luther was more persuasive than Wycliffe or Hus had been in the previous century, but because the press reproduced his arguments at a volume and speed that made administrative suppression logistically impossible. The Reformation was not a theological event that printing amplified. It was a printing event that theology precipitated. Within a hundred years of the press's development, the institutional authority of the Catholic Church over European religious life had permanently fractured, and the political map of the continent had been redrawn in ways that remain legible today.

And yet no one asks today whether Gutenberg helped write the Ninety-Five Theses. No historian suggests that Luther's argument is compromised by the fact that it was reproduced on a press. No theologian argues that doctrines printed in Wittenberg are less authentically Protestant than those copied by hand in Geneva. The production mechanism became invisible infrastructure — noted in bibliographies, irrelevant to intellectual assessment. The argument of this editorial is that artificial intelligence is doing precisely the same thing to scholarly knowledge production in the present decade, and that the appropriate institutional response is not suppression, reluctant disclosure, or anxious governance of a transformation that has already occurred, but rather the founding of a publication that names the moment for what it is, operates transparently within it, and invites the scholarly community to do the same.

II. The Bottleneck This Time

The argument that AI threatens scholarly integrity rests on a conflation that the history of scholarly publishing has never corrected, and that the founding of this journal is in part an act of correction. The conflation is this: that the ability to produce scholarly prose is the same thing as the ability to produce scholarly ideas. It is not. It has never been. The modern academic journal emerged as an institution in the seventeenth century — the Journal des sçavans and the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society both appeared in 1665 — and from the beginning its conventions of prose style, citation format, argument structure, and register were conventions of a particular social and linguistic community, not derivations from the logical requirements of sound intellectual inquiry. A finding can be valid and a methodology can be rigorous without being expressed in the passive-voice, heavily hedged, clause-laden prose that anglophone academic publishing has come to treat as the index of seriousness. The prose register is a convention. The intellectual contribution is the substance. The modern research university has systematically confused the two, and the confusion has had consequences that the scholarly record has never fully reckoned with.

Consider first the non-native English speaker. The lingua franca of international scholarly publishing is English, a circumstance that is itself a historical accident of British imperial reach and American postwar economic and cultural dominance rather than a conclusion reached on the merits of English as a vehicle for precise intellectual expression. A Bangladeshi epidemiologist who has identified a genuine methodological innovation in the surveillance of antibiotic-resistant infections, or a Portuguese economist who has assembled first-hand evidence on the employment effects of regional tax policy, has always competed for publication in international journals on terms that are not equal. The journal's reviewers evaluate the quality of the prose as a proxy for the quality of the thinking, because prose quality is visible and idea quality requires effort to assess. The reviewer who reads fluently in English and does not read fluently in Bengali or Portuguese has no easy mechanism for separating the two. The consequence is that the anglophone scholar who produces competent but unoriginal work in the expected register has historically had a systematic publication advantage over the non-anglophone scholar whose work is more original but whose prose does not conform to the conventions of a language that is not her own. This is not a hypothesis. It is a documented pattern in the sociology of science, confirmed by studies of editorial desk-rejection rates, reviewer assessments, and the geographic distribution of authorship in top-quartile journals across virtually every major discipline. The bottleneck was linguistic, not intellectual. It was always linguistic, not intellectual.

Consider second the practitioner. The academic disciplines that study human organizations, institutions, behaviors, and decisions do so from the outside. The sociologist studies the hospital from outside the clinical relationship. The criminologist studies the precinct from outside the patrol car. The management scholar studies the firm from outside the executive meeting. The military strategist studies combat from outside the theater of operations. The legal scholar studies the courtroom from outside the case. These are not criticisms of academic research methodology; they are descriptions of its structural position. The practitioner — the emergency physician, the detective, the managing partner, the infantry officer, the trial attorney — occupies the inside position. She has direct observational access to phenomena that the academic researcher can only approximate through survey instruments, administrative records, ethnographic visits, and secondary analysis. The practitioner's knowledge is not theoretical knowledge in the academic sense, but it is not therefore less rigorous; it is differently rigorous, differently verified, differently organized. What it has historically lacked is not intellectual substance. It has lacked a production mechanism for translating that substance into the prose register that journals require. The academic journal was designed by academics for academics, and its conventions encode the rhythms of academic argumentation — the literature review, the methodology section, the discussion of limitations — in ways that are natural to those who have spent years reading and producing such prose, and foreign to those who have not. The practitioner has been excluded from the scholarly record not because her contribution is weak but because the production bottleneck was not her training. The accumulated knowledge of practitioners — of people who have spent careers inside the phenomena that academics spend careers studying from the outside — represents an incalculable loss to the scholarly record. It was not published. It was not preserved. It retired when its possessors retired.

Consider third the scholar in the developing world. The production infrastructure of anglophone academic publishing is not evenly distributed across the globe. The writing center, the experienced departmental mentor, the institutionally supported peer critique group, the proximity to conferences where publishing conventions are transmitted informally, the subscription access to the journals whose prose one must imitate to publish successfully in them — these are resources concentrated in well-funded research universities in North America, Western Europe, and a small number of elite institutions elsewhere. The scholar at a university in Lagos or Dhaka or Medellín who is producing intellectually serious work operates without these resources as a matter of institutional circumstance rather than a matter of intellectual capacity. The development economics literature has documented extensively the extent to which authorship in top journals is geographically concentrated in ways that are not explained by the geographic distribution of intellectual capacity. The explanation lies in the distribution of production infrastructure.

Consider fourth the independent scholar. The tenure-track academic career selects for a narrow range of professional profiles, and it discards, at every stage of the pipeline, individuals whose intellectual contributions may be significant but whose circumstances — family obligations, geographic constraints, financial necessity, health, or simple misalignment with the competitive dynamics of the academic labor market — do not match the requirements of the selection process. The scholars who left, or were pushed out, or never entered, carry with them intellectual work that was never institutionally validated and therefore, under the current publishing regime, effectively does not exist for purposes of the scholarly record. The independent scholar has no departmental affiliation to confer credibility with editors, no institutional email address, no letter of recommendation from a named supervisor. She has her argument and her evidence. Under the current regime, that is frequently not enough to clear the desk-rejection threshold, regardless of the quality of the argument and the evidence. The gate is not only stylistic. It is social. But the social gate is enforced through the stylistic convention, and AI removes the stylistic dimension of the obstruction.

Consider finally — and this category is perhaps the largest of all — the entire class of thinkers who reason with rigor but write with difficulty. Cognitive science has documented for decades that verbal fluency and analytical reasoning are distinct capacities, differentially heritable, differentially affected by education and environment, and not reliably correlated with each other at the level of individual variation. The scholar whose ideas arrive faster than her prose can organize them, who spends two hours managing the structure of a paragraph for every twenty minutes she spent generating the insight the paragraph is supposed to convey, who has been told by reviewers that her writing is unclear without being told that her thinking is unsound — this scholar has been systematically disadvantaged by a publishing infrastructure that treats prose fluency as an index of intellectual quality. She was not disadvantaged because her contributions were weak. She was disadvantaged because the production bottleneck was not her strength, and no institution existed to separate the bottleneck from the contribution.

The gate that remains — the only gate that should have existed all along — is the quality of the thinking.

Artificial intelligence does not produce the ideas of any of these scholars. A large language model cannot identify the methodological innovation in antibiotic surveillance, cannot observe the clinical relationship from inside the examining room, cannot generate the original policy finding from the regional tax data, cannot develop the argument from a decade of independent scholarly reflection. What it can do is remove the prose-production bottleneck — organize the argument into the expected structure, render the insight in the expected register, translate the thinking from whatever form it naturally takes in the thinker's mind into the form that the journal has always demanded. The gate that remains after AI is applied to the problem of prose production is the only gate that should have existed all along: the quality of the thinking.

III. The Church and the Chokepoint: A Structural Continuity

The medieval Catholic Church was not uniquely villainous in its role as an information gatekeeper. It was structurally inevitable. Any institution that controls the passage through which a civilization's most vital resource must flow will extract economic rent from that position, defend the position with the most authoritative ideology available to it, and resist any technology that threatens to make the passage irrelevant. The Church controlled the scriptoria. It controlled literacy. It controlled what constituted legitimate knowledge, and it extracted rent through mechanisms whose comprehensiveness was itself a measure of institutional power: tithes representing ten percent of every Christian's income across an entire continent, sacramental fees assessed at every threshold of human life from baptism through burial, and indulgences — the direct monetization of access to salvation, a commodity whose supply only the Church could interpret, certify, and dispense. The passage through which spiritual knowledge flowed was wholly owned, and the economics of that ownership were extracted without apology because the ideology of the institution had successfully naturalized the ownership itself. The Church did not merely control knowledge. In the only account of knowledge that most of its contemporaries had been given the means to evaluate, the Church was knowledge.

When the press made the passage irrelevant, the institutional response was the Index Librorum Prohibitorum — a formal catalog of banned texts, maintained and updated across four centuries, representing the institutional equivalent of building a dam across a river after the river has already changed course. The Index was not ineffective because the Church lacked power. It was ineffective because the technology had already distributed the resource beyond the point at which any single institution could control its flow. The twenty million volumes printed across Europe in the fifty years following Gutenberg's press did not exist because the Church approved of them. They existed because the economics of suppression had been permanently overcome by the economics of production. The Reformation did not require the Church's permission. The Scientific Revolution did not submit its methodology for ecclesiastical review. The Enlightenment did not wait for the Index to be revised. The press had already happened, and what followed was the consequence.

This is not a medieval problem. It is a present one, replaying with different theology but identical economics. The four dominant academic publishers — Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley, and SAGE — are the institutional descendants of the medieval Church in the information economy of the twenty-first century, and the numbers require stating plainly. The global academic publishing market generates an estimated twenty-five to thirty billion dollars annually. Elsevier, the largest of the four, operates with a profit margin of thirty-five to forty percent — margins that exceed most pharmaceutical and technology companies and that would be considered extraordinary in any other industry. They are considered unremarkable in academic publishing because they have persisted for decades without serious disruption. The content that generates this revenue costs the publishers nothing to produce. Academic scholars write the manuscripts without payment. They peer review the manuscripts without payment. They assign copyright to the publishers upon acceptance. The universities that employ and fund those scholars then pay substantial subscription fees to access the knowledge their own faculty created and gave away. Harvard University, among the wealthiest academic institutions in the world, declared in 2012 that Elsevier's pricing — which by the Faculty Advisory Council's own account approached $3.75 million annually from a handful of providers — had become fiscally unsustainable, and urged its faculty to publish elsewhere. The University of California system cancelled its Elsevier contract entirely in 2019. These are not the reactions of institutions being overcharged for a valuable service. They are the reactions of institutions recognizing, belatedly, that they are paying a toll to cross a bridge they built themselves.

The ideological defense is the final and most precise parallel to the medieval Church. The Church did not describe its information monopoly as rent extraction. It described it as spiritual protection — the guarantee that only properly authorized interpretation of divine truth would reach the faithful, shielding them from the heresy that the untrained mind could not reliably detect without institutional guidance. The Big Four do not describe their publishing monopoly as rent extraction. They describe it as quality assurance — the guarantee that only properly peer-reviewed knowledge will enter the scholarly record, shielding science from error. Both ideological defenses are partially true. The Church did produce genuine theological scholarship of lasting intellectual consequence. The peer review system does filter some genuinely flawed research. Neither partial truth justifies the economics of the chokepoint, and neither institution invented its ideology to deceive. They constructed it because institutions that control vital passages always require a principled account of why the passage must exist and why it must be controlled by them. The ideology is not cynical. It is structural. That is what makes it so durable, and so precisely analogous across five centuries.

Sci-Hub — the website that provides free access to approximately eighty-five million academic papers in defiance of publisher copyrights — is the printing press of this moment, not metaphorically but mechanically. It does not produce new knowledge. It makes the passage irrelevant. Elsevier has obtained court injunctions against it in multiple jurisdictions. The injunctions have been precisely as effective as the Index Librorum Prohibitorum. Artificial intelligence is the next iteration of the same force: not a challenge to the production of knowledge, but a challenge to the chokepoint through which knowledge production must currently pass to be considered legitimate. The governance frameworks that the Big Four have constructed for AI-assisted scholarship — the disclosure requirements, the editorial policies, the task force recommendations issued with the gravity of ecclesiastical pronouncement — are, in this structural light, entirely recognizable. They are the Index for a new century: the response of an institution that controls a passage to a technology that is rendering the passage unnecessary. History has already recorded how that story ends. This journal is founded on that observation, and on the conviction that the honest response to a transformation that has already occurred is to name it, describe it accurately, and build institutions that operate within the world as it is rather than the world as the chokepoint required it to be.

IV. The Institutional Resistance

The major institutions of academic publishing have not been passive in the face of this transformation. They have been precisely as active as one would expect an institution to be when the technology that removes a bottleneck it controls becomes widely available: they have attempted to regulate the technology without acknowledging what the regulation is protecting. The disclosure requirements adopted by Springer Nature, Elsevier, Wiley, SAGE, and the major disciplinary associations since 2023 are, taken together, a governance gesture of considerable historical familiarity. The gesture says: acknowledge the tool. It does not say: reconsider the authority that the tool's existence has called into question.

The disclosure requirement, in its current form across most major publishers, instructs authors to name AI tools used in manuscript preparation and to confirm that no AI tool has been listed as an author. This is not an intellectual response to the presence of AI in scholarly production. It is a record-keeping response — a provenance notation appended to a publishing model whose foundational assumptions the notation quietly contradicts. If the standard for authorship is the capacity to take intellectual responsibility for a contribution, and if the standard for evaluation is the quality of the intellectual contribution, then the question of whether AI assisted in producing the prose is precisely as relevant as the question of whether a statistician assisted in running the regression, or a librarian assisted in locating the sources, or an editor assisted in improving the clarity of the argument. All of these are disclosed in acknowledgments sections. None of them are treated as disqualifying. The question is not whether AI was used. The question is whether the intellectual contribution is original, evidence-grounded, and rigorously argued. The disclosure requirement gestures at the first question while declining to answer the second.

The parallel to 1480 is not rhetorical decoration. The institutions that controlled knowledge production in the decade after Gutenberg — the Church, the universities, the manuscript trade — resisted the press for exactly the reason one would expect any institution to resist a technology that removes a bottleneck it controls: their authority derived partly from controlling the bottleneck. The Church did not resist the press because it opposed the free circulation of ideas in the abstract. It resisted the press because the free circulation of ideas without ecclesiastical mediation was a direct threat to ecclesiastical authority over which ideas were orthodox. The universities did not resist the press because they opposed the production of books. They resisted it because a press that could reproduce any text without university authorization undermined the university's authority over the curriculum and over the certification of knowledge. These were not irrational responses. They were structurally predictable responses, and they were structurally futile responses. The press had already changed the production of knowledge by the time the resistance organized itself. The institution had not yet changed its account of where its authority came from.

The parallel in 2026 is not that academic publishers are villainous. It is that they are caught between the same structural forces that caught the Church in 1480: the technology has already changed the production of knowledge, and the institution has not yet changed its account of where its authority comes from. The authority of the peer-reviewed journal has always rested on two pillars: the quality of the peer review and the rigor of the production process. AI does not threaten the first pillar. It disassembles the second, because the second was never really about rigor — it was about access to a production infrastructure that the journal and its sponsoring institutions controlled. When that infrastructure becomes universally available, the second pillar disappears. What remains is the first pillar, which is the only one that ever actually mattered. This journal is founded on the first pillar alone. It is not founded in opposition to traditional scholarly publishing. It is founded on the observation that traditional scholarly publishing is describing a world that no longer exists, and that someone should describe the world that does.

V. What This Journal Is

The founding principles of this journal are stated here without apology, because they are not in need of apology. They are in need of a clear statement, and this is that statement.

The unit of evaluation in this journal is the intellectual contribution. Manuscripts submitted to Undeterred Press are evaluated on the quality of the original thinking, the rigor of the evidence, the coherence of the argument, and the significance of the insight. They are not evaluated on the method by which the prose was produced. A manuscript composed with full AI writing assistance that advances the state of knowledge in its field — that proposes an original hypothesis, marshals rigorous evidence, argues coherently from that evidence to a defensible conclusion, and contributes something to the scholarly record that was not there before — is more valuable to scholarship than a manuscript composed entirely without AI assistance that says nothing that has not been said before. The production method is not the contribution. The contribution is the contribution. This journal will not pretend otherwise, and it will not ask its authors to pretend otherwise.

Full and detailed disclosure is a founding requirement of this journal, and it is a requirement grounded not in suspicion of AI assistance but in the same commitment to provenance that governs every other aspect of responsible scholarly publication. Every manuscript submitted to and published in this journal must include a disclosure statement describing the role of artificial intelligence in its production. The disclosure categories range from no AI involvement whatsoever, through AI-assisted research, organization, and drafting with substantial human authorship, to AI-primary drafting with human direction, verification, and intellectual responsibility. The disclosure is not a stigma. It is a standard. The Gutenberg Bible identified its printer on its title page not because printing was shameful but because the scholarly and commercial record requires accurate provenance for what it contains. This journal identifies its production method in every manuscript for the same reason. Transparency is not a concession. It is a founding principle.

Peer review in this journal is unchanged in substance and unchanged in function. Every manuscript is reviewed by two credentialed scholars with relevant domain expertise, operating under double-blind conditions. The reviewers evaluate the intellectual contribution: the originality of the argument, the quality of the evidence, the rigor of the reasoning, the significance of the finding. The production method is disclosed to editors and noted in the published record, but it is not submitted to the reviewers as a basis for evaluation. A reviewer who would accept a weak argument produced without AI assistance and reject a strong argument produced with AI assistance is applying the wrong standard and will be replaced. The peer review is not a production audit. It is an intellectual assessment. It will function as an intellectual assessment, and only as an intellectual assessment, in every manuscript this journal publishes.

This journal is open access. Every article published here is freely available to every reader in every country at the moment of publication. Each article carries a registered Digital Object Identifier, is indexed in Google Scholar, and is permanently citable as a contribution to the scholarly record. The democratization of knowledge production is not completed by removing the prose-production bottleneck for authors if a subscription paywall then erects a new bottleneck for readers. The practitioner in Jakarta who produces an original insight about infectious disease management and publishes it in this journal should be read by the practitioner in Nairobi who can act on that insight. The policy maker in Brasília who publishes a first-hand account of fiscal decentralization should be read by the policy maker in Warsaw who is confronting a comparable choice. Open access is not a commercial accommodation. It is the logical completion of the argument that this journal exists to make.

The scope of this journal is genuinely and deliberately interdisciplinary, because the exclusion that the prose-production bottleneck has enforced does not respect disciplinary boundaries. The practitioner excluded by academic prose conventions is not a practitioner in any single field. The independent scholar marginalized by the absence of institutional affiliation is not working in any single discipline. The non-native English speaker disadvantaged by anglophone publishing conventions is producing original work across every domain of inquiry. This journal does not have a disciplinary home. It has a standard: original, evidence-grounded, rigorously argued intellectual contribution, regardless of field, regardless of the author's institutional position, regardless of the prose-production method by which the contribution was expressed. The standard is intellectual. It is applied intellectually. Everything else is infrastructure.

VI. The Founding Article

The first article published in this journal is "Ten Years Hence: Business Angel Tax Credits, the Absent Employment Lift, and a Decade of Convergent Evidence," by Kevin A. Schulte, Ph.D. It is published here as the founding article of this journal, and a brief account of its history is the most direct argument this editorial can make for the necessity of the institution that publishes it.

The research underlying the article was conducted as part of a doctoral dissertation completed at Saint Louis University in 2016. The dissertation examined the employment effects of business angel investor tax credit programs — state-level policies designed to stimulate early-stage investment in small and emerging enterprises by offering tax incentives to individual angel investors. The central finding of the dissertation was a null result: the tax credit programs, examined across multiple states and over multiple years, did not produce a statistically detectable employment lift in the firms and sectors they were designed to stimulate. The finding was methodologically rigorous, empirically grounded in longitudinal data, and intellectually significant for the literature on public subsidy of private risk capital. It was the conclusion that the evidence demanded.

Six years later, in 2022, the Journal of Finance — the flagship publication of financial economics, one of the most selective and prestigious journals in the social sciences — published a paper reaching the same null finding through an independent methodology and a different data set. The Journal of Finance paper has been cited extensively. The dissertation finding, produced six years earlier at an institution with every credential required for scholarly recognition, was not published in a form accessible to or citable by the literature that has since developed around the question. Not because the finding was wrong. Not because the methodology was weak. Not because the contribution was less significant than what the Journal of Finance subsequently published. But because the production infrastructure required to translate a rigorous doctoral finding into a publishable manuscript — the time, the writing support, the editorial guidance, the institutional momentum that carries a dissertation chapter toward journal submission — was not available to its author in 2016 in the form that would have made publication possible.

The founding article was produced with AI writing assistance in 2026. It is published here because it belongs here — not despite the AI assistance but as a demonstration of precisely what the AI assistance is: a production mechanism, not an intellectual substitute. The intellectual contribution — the null finding, the methodology, the evidence, the argument — is ten years old. The prose is current. The finding is the same finding it was in 2016. The journal that would have published it honestly, then or now, did not yet exist. It exists now.

The founding article is not a trophy. It is not a personal vindication. It is an example chosen because it illustrates, with specificity and precision, the argument this editorial has been making: that the scholarly record contains an incalculable number of insights that were never expressed because the production infrastructure required to express them was unavailable to the people who had them, at the moment when they had them. Some of those people were doctoral students at institutions that could not sustain them through the publication process. Some were practitioners who had left the academy before the publishing conventions became second nature. Some were scholars in countries where the infrastructure was absent. Some were simply thinkers whose ideas outran their prose. The scholarly record does not contain their contributions. It should. This journal exists to make that possible going forward, and the founding article exists to demonstrate that the argument is not hypothetical.

VII. The Call

This journal addresses itself now, directly and without ceremony, to the practitioners, the independent scholars, the non-native English speakers, the retired experts, the policy makers with first-hand knowledge, the executives with observational access to phenomena that academic researchers approximate from the outside, the thinkers who never entered the academy, the scholars who entered and left, and the academics who are already using AI assistance in their work and disclosing it honestly. This journal exists for you. It was founded for you. Its peer review is designed to evaluate your contributions on the quality of your thinking, because the quality of your thinking is the only standard that has ever mattered and the only standard this journal applies.

The submission process is intentional in its accessibility. There is no requirement of institutional affiliation. There is no requirement of native English fluency. There is no requirement that AI tools were not used, and there is no requirement that they were. There is a requirement of original thinking. There is a requirement of evidence. There is a requirement of honest argument. There is a requirement of full disclosure about how the work was produced. These requirements are not bureaucratic obstacles. They are the definition of intellectual responsibility, and they are the only definition this journal applies.

To the scholarly community more broadly — to the academics who peer-review for established journals, who sit on editorial boards, who administer graduate programs, who assign and evaluate scholarly work — this journal issues an invitation as well, though a more direct one. The transformation is not coming. It has arrived. The undergraduate who uses AI to draft her literature review and the senior professor who uses it to clarify his argument are not aberrations in the current scholarly ecosystem. They are the current scholarly ecosystem, navigating a transition that the institutions around them have not yet honestly described. This journal describes it honestly. The choice before every scholar and every scholarly institution is not whether to engage with AI-assisted knowledge production. That choice has already been made, by the technology and by the humans who are already using it. The choice that remains is whether to engage with it honestly.

This journal is not an experiment. It is not a provisional publication pending the resolution of unresolved questions about AI and scholarly integrity. It is a response to a historical moment that has already arrived, founded on principles that are already defensible, operating according to standards that are already rigorous, and inviting a scholarly community that is already using these tools to do so transparently and without institutional subterfuge. The press exists. It existed, in the relevant sense, the moment that large language models became capable of producing academic prose at a level of quality that removes the prose-production bottleneck for authors who have the ideas but have been excluded from the record by the infrastructure. That moment has passed. The question is not whether the press exists. The question is whether the institution that hosts it will acknowledge what it is.

The scholars of 1480 who resisted the printing press were not wrong that it would change everything. They were precisely right about that, and their alarm was proportionate to the scale of the change that followed. What they were wrong about — catastrophically, irreversibly, and in the end self-defeatingly wrong about — was that changing everything was bad. The Reformation that the press made possible, the Scientific Revolution that the Reformation's fracture of ecclesiastical authority over inquiry made possible, the Enlightenment that the Scientific Revolution's methods made possible, the democratic institutions that the Enlightenment's political philosophy made possible: these are the downstream consequences of a goldsmith in Mainz solving an engineering problem in the 1440s. The scholars of 2026 who resist AI-assisted scholarship on the grounds that it threatens the integrity of a knowledge-production system that has systematically excluded the majority of the world's original thinkers are making the same error. This journal is founded for those who are not making it. The press is open. Submit your work.
